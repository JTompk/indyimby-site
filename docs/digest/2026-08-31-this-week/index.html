This week in Indy entitlement — August 31, 2026

Two hearings this week — BZA I on Tuesday, the Metropolitan Development Commission on Wednesday — and on paper it’s a quiet one: 24 items, zero new filings since last Monday. But the MDC’s consent agenda has more in it than the count suggests. Here’s what matters.

The headline: TWG’s six stories at 412 West McCarty

2026-REG-015 is the largest housing item Indianapolis has heard in weeks: a six-story, mixed-use building at 412 West McCarty Street and two Chadwick Street parcels, steps from Lucas Oil Stadium. TWG is proposing 270 dwelling units, roughly 6,500 square feet of ground-floor commercial and retail, and 204 spaces in an integrated garage.

Two things worth noticing. First, this is not a rezoning. The site is already CBD-2 with the Regional Center overlay, so the use is permitted by right and what the Commission is reviewing is Regional Center design approval — massing, frontage, how the building meets the street. That’s the entitlement process working the way it should: the zoning already said yes, and the hearing is about how, not whether. Second, the parking. 204 spaces for 270 units is about three-quarters of a space per home, in a location where a resident can walk to the stadium, the convention district, and the Cultural Trail. A decade ago a downtown project at this ratio would have drawn a fight. This week it’s on the no-appeal list, recommended for approval.

The sleeper: the city is buying a catalog of pre-approved house plans

Buried in the policy resolutions, 2026-P-010 authorizes DMD to contract with Opticos Design for up to $252,500 to produce “residential pre-reviewed architectural plans.”

If you follow housing policy, Opticos needs no introduction — it’s the Berkeley firm that coined the term “missing middle.” Pre-reviewed plans are a tool a growing number of cities use to take friction out of small-scale infill: the city commissions a set of house, duplex, and small-multiplex designs, runs them through plan review once, and then anyone building on an eligible lot can pull the design off the shelf and skip most of the architectural and review cost. For the small builders and owner-developers who do the bulk of Indianapolis infill, that is often the difference between a project penciling and not.

The contract doesn’t tell us which lot types, which zoning districts, or how many plans. Those details will decide whether this is a brochure or a real accelerant. It’s the item on this week’s agenda most worth following into next year.
